The movement

It began as a material.
It became a philosophy.

In 1953, Le Corbusier used the phrase béton brut — raw concrete — to describe his Unité d'Habitation in Marseille. He meant it literally: the formwork was stripped away, and whatever the concrete looked like, you left it alone. No plaster, no paint, no apology.

His followers heard something larger. By the 1960s, an entire generation of architects — in London, Boston, Belgrade, Montreal, Tokyo — was building out of raw concrete and building boldly. The critics called it brutalism. The architects called it honest.

The idea

Honesty was the whole point.

Brutalism was not interested in beauty in the traditional sense. It was interested in truth. If the building was made of concrete, you saw the concrete — its texture, its pour lines, its imperfections. If structure held the floors, you saw the structure. There was no decorative shell, no cladding, no comfortable lie.

Its critics called it inhuman. Its defenders called it the most human architecture ever built: one that refused to pretend to be something it wasn't. What you see is what it is. The building stands there, in the weather, and does not apologize.

"We were concerned with the ethical use of materials — not a style of materials, but an attitude toward them."
— Alison & Peter Smithson, 1956

It is still out there, in the rain, unashamed.
