A One-Act Play, 1944

No Exit Huis Clos

Jean-Paul Sartre

scroll ↓

The Setting

A drawing room, in Second Empire style.

A massive bronze ornament stands on the mantelpiece. There are three sofas — one vinous, one blue, one green. The lights never go out. There are no windows. There are no mirrors. The door is locked, and the bell does not always ring when pressed.

Three strangers are brought in, one by one, by a polite Valet. They expected racks and pitchforks. Instead they get this room, and each other. Forever.

"You remember all we were told about the torture-chambers, the fire and brimstone, the 'burning marl.' Old wives' tales!"

Dramatis Personae

The Three Damned.

Each was sent here for a reason. Each will refuse, at first, to say what it was. They will, eventually, be made to say.

"
I am too heavy. How can the dead be so heavy?
— Inès, on the room

The door flies open. They could leave. None of them moves.

The Revelation

There are no instruments of torture.

No racks. No flames. No leering devils. Sartre's hell is administrative — a hotel room with the bell broken, the lights stuck on, and the company poorly chosen. The torturer of each is simply the other two. They will watch. They will judge. They will never sleep, and never look away.

This is the trick of the play, and the trick of Sartre's philosophy: the self does not exist in private. You are what you appear to be in someone else's eyes — and once you are dead, that picture is finished, and you cannot retouch it.

Garcin is a coward because Inès has decided he is one. There is no court of appeal.

L'enfer, c'est les autres.

Hell is other people.

Garcin, Scene V
